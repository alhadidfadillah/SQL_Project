# Major League Baseball Analysis

## About Data
A large amount of historical player data, including player statistics such as schools attended, salaries, teams played for, height, weight, and more.

## The Objectives
Track how player statistics have changed over time and across different teams in the league.

## The Approaches
1. Analyze the data from the school table.
2. Analyze the data from the salary table.
3. Do player career analysis.
4. Do player comparison analysis from the player table.

## Key Findings
- From the 1860s until the 2010s, 3265 schools have produced Major League Baseball players.
- The University of Texas at Austin, the University of Southern California, and Arizona State University are the 3 schools that produced the most players, with a total of 107, 105, and 101, respectively.
- SFG is the highest team with the most average annual spending for salaries, with a total of 143.5 million.
- Over the years, there have been a lot of teams that have surpassed 1 billion in cumulative spending for salaries. The NYA was the first team to surpass it back then in 2003.
- Nicholas is the player who has the longest career (35 years) in Major League Baseball.
- 19 players started and ended on the same team and also played for over a decade.
- The average height and weight of MLB players have almost always increased over the years.
- Each team generally has players who bat more right-handed than left-handed. However, some players bat with both; the SFG team has the highest percentage of players who bat with both.

## Recommendations
- Deepen partnerships with schools that produced the most players (The University of Texas at Austin, the University of Southern California, and Arizona State University) for Player Development & Scouting. Collaborate with their coaching staff on development programs, place more scouts in their conferences, and use their success to understand the traits of a "big-league ready" player.
- Analyze the habits and profiles of players with extreme longevity (like Nicholas). What are their physical conditioning routines, playing positions, and injury histories? This can inform modern training and load management.
- Scouting and player development models should account for the physical frame of modern athletes. This doesn't mean excluding smaller players, but understanding how to project the physical development of younger prospects to meet the modern game's demands.
- NYA was the first to surpass $1B in cumulative salary, and many teams have since followed. For large-market teams (like NYA, LAD, BOS), this is a benchmark of financial commitment. For mid- and small-market teams, the key is to analyze how the big spenders allocated their funds. Did they buy superstar hitters? Lock down elite pitching? This analysis is crucial for competing efficiently with a smaller budget.
- 19 players spent their entire >10-year career with one team. These players are a marketing goldmine. Teams should actively celebrate their current long-tenured players and share their stories extensively. This builds an emotional connection with the fanbase, making the team feel like a family rather than just a business.
- Players have gotten bigger, heavier, and the game has become more specialized (e.g., more switch hitters). Create engaging content—infographics, video series, articles—for MLB.com and social media showing "The Evolution of the MLB Player." This educates fans and adds a layer of historical depth to the viewing experience, highlighting how the game has changed.
